Search is turning into an answer engine. Whether the answer comes from a classic results page with an AI overview, or a chatbot quoting the web, the pattern is the same: the machine reads several sources, then answers the user itself. The site that gets cited is the one the machine could read *confidently*.

## What confident reading means

### Say something specific

A page that states a price, a process, a timeline or a trade-off in plain sentences is easy to quote. A page that says "solutions tailored to your needs" is not. Specificity has always been good writing; now it is also machine-readable.

### Keep the structure boring

One `<h1>` per page, headings that describe what the section actually says, and paragraphs under them. It reads well, and it gives the answering machine a clean outline to work from.

### Mark up the facts

Structured data does not rank a page by itself, but it removes ambiguity about what a page *is*: an article, a product, an FAQ, a job posting. This site emits it for [every service](/services), every [rate card](/pricing) and every [role](/careers) — the same principle applies to any site we [build](/services/web).

## What to stop doing

- **Writing for the keyword, not the question.** Ten thin pages targeting ten keyword variants lose to one thorough page on the topic.
- **Hiding the answer.** A page that buries its useful fact below four screens of preamble teaches the reader — and the machine — that the page is mostly filler.
- **Measuring only rankings.** If your traffic shifts from clicks to citations, the numbers that matter are branded search volume and direct visits, not position seven.

## The uncomfortable summary

None of this is new advice. Write clearly, structure honestly, say things worth quoting. What has changed is that the audience includes a reader that never gets tired, never gets charmed by the brand, and leaves the moment the text stops being concrete.
